# Discovery and story

Sources: Figma Resource Library, *User-centered design questions*, *What is UX design*, *Storytelling in design*. Paraphrased.

Generic sites come from generic inputs. Most AI tells disappear when the page is built from real answers about real visitors. Ask before designing.

## 1. Discovery questions

Ask in one message. Keep it short; skipped answers are inferred and stated back.

**Essential (always ask):**
1. **Who is it for?** Who visits, and what do they already know? First-timers or returning?
2. **What are they trying to do?** The one main action (book, buy, call, sign up, visit, read). How do they do it today, and what annoys them about that?
3. **What's real?** Photos, screenshots, real numbers, real quotes with names, logos you may use. (Nothing gets invented.)
4. **What should it feel like?** Pick a direction with an edge (editorial, brutal, soft, utilitarian, luxurious, playful, technical, austere). "Clean and modern" is not a direction. Plus any brand colour or font already owned.

**Ask when relevant:**
- **Context:** device, place, hurry, mood. Accessibility needs?
- **Evidence:** analytics, reviews, support tickets, feedback you already have?
- **Success:** how will you know the site worked?
- **Constraints:** existing brand, stack, content or pages that must stay?
- **Sites you admire**, and what specifically about them?

Rules:
- Ask about visitors' tasks, not about features.
- Open questions, not leading ones.
- Anything you invent (personas, copy, positioning) is a hypothesis until the user confirms it. Say which parts you assumed.
- Find the root cause behind a stated problem; different causes need different fixes.
- Design for the less-than-ideal case: slow connection, small screen, first visit, tired visitor.

## 2. Build the page as a story

Before writing sections, list the questions a visitor arrives with, in order. Then order the sections to answer them.

Typical visitor questions: *Where am I? Is this for me? What exactly do I get? Can I trust it? What does it cost / take? How do I start?*

| Beat | Job | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening** (hero) | Say plainly who you are and what you offer, in one concrete line. The service and the place, not a slogan | A vague tagline ("Elevate your workflow") |
| **Hook** | Show the real situation: the visitor's problem or the product doing its one job | Abstract illustrations |
| **The offer** | Show the actual things: menu, products, services, the plan, the work | A grid of three generic "features" |
| **Proof** | Real evidence, placed near the decision it supports | Invented stats and logo walls |
| **Details** | Logistics and fine print (price, shipping, hours, security) where the decision happens | Everything dumped in the hero |
| **The action** | One clear next step that names what happens | "Get started" / "Learn more" |
| **After** | What happens next: delivery, support, reasons to come back | Ignored |

Rules:
- **Cut ruthlessly.** Every section must move the story forward. Leaving things out is a design decision.
- Each section has one clear next step.
- The headings alone, read in order, should tell the story.
- Build from the anatomy of the category: a restaurant site is built around the menu, hours and booking; a band around dates and music; a consultancy around the work and how to hire. Not around a generic hero / features / testimonials / CTA template.

## Audit checks

- Five-second test: could a first-time visitor say what this is and what to do next?
- Read only the headings in order. Do they tell a coherent story that answers the visitor's questions in sequence?
- Find filler that doesn't advance the story: generic feature grids, vague slogans, duplicate CTAs.
- Count the steps to complete the main task.
